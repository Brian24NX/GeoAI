The big picture

What is this project about?

Imagine you're using Google Maps for a hiking trail. It tells you "this hike takes 3 hours." But how does it know that?

The answer: it uses a formula from 1993 written by a guy named Waldo Tobler. His formula says: the steeper the hill, the slower you walk. Simple.

The problem? He never actually measured real hikers. He just copied some numbers from a Swiss textbook. And 30 years later, everyone still uses his formula without questioning it.

Think of it this way: imagine if every restaurant in the world set their cooking times based on one recipe book from 1993, and nobody ever checked if the times were actually right. That's what's happening in geography right now with hiking speed.

Your job: test it with real data

You're going to download real GPS tracks from thousands of actual hikers, measure how fast they really walked, and see if Tobler's formula is right or wrong.

But here's the twist that makes it a remote sensing project...

The twist

Why is this a remote sensing project?

Some trails on OpenStreetMap have labels like:

🪨
Surface: gravel
📏
Width: 2m
⛰️
Difficulty: hard

These labels predict hiking speed really well. But here's the catch: most trails in the world don't have these labels.

So the big question is:

Can we look at the ground from satellites and LiDAR (laser scanning from airplanes) and figure out the same information?

If yes → we can predict hiking speed anywhere on Earth, even where no map exists.

The experiment

The 4-arm test (the core of your paper)

Think of it as giving the same exam to 4 students, but each student gets different cheat sheets:

Arm 1: Tobler
Only sees: slope
"How fast using the 1993 formula?"
Arm 2: Map tags
Sees: slope + trail labels
"How fast if we have a good map?"
Arm 3: Remote sensing
Sees: slope + satellite/LiDAR
"How fast from the sky alone?"
Arm 4: Everything
Sees: all of the above
"How fast with all info?"

The winning outcome: Arm 3 (satellites only) scores close to Arm 2 (map labels). That would mean satellites can replace missing map data — a big deal.

Even if Arm 3 fails badly, that's still a useful result. It would tell the field exactly which trail features matter for speed but are invisible from above.

Data cleaning

What does "cleaning the GPS traces" actually mean?

When someone uploads a GPS track from their hike, the raw data is messy. Here's what you have to fix:

🍕
Remove stops
Lunch breaks, photo stops, resting — they make speed look artificially slow
📍
Map-match
Snap GPS points onto the actual trail (GPS drifts under trees)
🚴
Detect mode
Was this person walking, biking, or on horseback?
⬆️⬇️
Get direction
Were they going uphill or downhill on this segment?

This is the hardest and most time-consuming part. The semester plan literally says: "Read section 8 twice. It's the project."

Checkpoints

The 2 gates (go or no-go decisions)

Gate 1 — Week 3

"Can we even use this data?"

When people upload GPS tracks to OpenStreetMap, they pick a privacy level. Some levels scramble the time order of the points. Without time order, you can't calculate speed. If most tracks in your region are scrambled → pick a different region.

Gate 2 — Week 11

"Did the satellites help?"

Do your learned models beat Tobler's 1993 formula? Does remote sensing get close to what map labels give you? If nothing beats Tobler, that's a surprising finding worth publishing honestly.

Key terms cheat sheet

Vocabulary you'll hear in the meeting

T

Tobler's function — the 1993 formula: speed = 6 × e^(-3.5 × |slope + 0.05|). The thing you're testing.

L

LiDAR — laser scanning from airplanes. Gives you 3D shape of the ground and trees at 1-meter resolution.

O

OSM — OpenStreetMap. Where the GPS hiking tracks come from. Also has trail labels (when they exist).

M

Map-matching — snapping messy GPS points onto the actual trail on the map. Hard because GPS drifts under tree cover.

H

Heteroscedastic — fancy word meaning "the model predicts both the speed AND how uncertain it is about that speed."

3

3DEP — a US government program that provides 1-meter elevation data. You need to verify it covers your region.

N

NAIP — aerial photos of the US at 1-meter resolution, 4 color bands (red, green, blue, near-infrared).

I

IGARSS — the conference you're submitting to. In Reykjavík, Iceland, July 2027. 4-page paper format.
