1 · The one-minute version

Three ideas hold the whole project.

The problem

GIS software still estimates walking speed with Tobler's 1993 formula. It was fit to old marching tables, not to real hikes, and says you walk 6 km/h on a gentle downhill. Real hikers are much slower, and the formula knows nothing about rocks, roots or vegetation.

The data

Hikers upload GPS tracks to OpenStreetMap. We turned thousands of them into measured 50 m stretches of trail, each with a real speed and slope, across US/Canadian mountain regions so far.

The question

Maps sometimes say how hard or rough a trail is (OSM "tags"), but usually don't. Can remote sensing fill that gap? Airborne lidar and satellite embeddings, ideally even in a region the model has never seen.

What you'll have at the end of the semester: a 4-page paper for IGARSS 2027 (the big IEEE remote-sensing conference, Reykjavík, July 2027), the multi-region dataset behind it, and the code: all in your GitHub repo Brian24NX/GeoAI.

2 · Why anyone cares

"How long will it take to get from A to B on foot?" sounds simple. Many decisions depend on the answer, especially in the backcountry, where maps are thin.

🚁 Search & rescue

How far could a lost hiker have gone in 6 hours? The search area grows with the square of travel speed.

🔥 Wildfire safety

Escape routes for firefighters and evacuation planning need realistic travel times. Campbell's group at the US Forest Service works on exactly this.

📱 Trail apps

"3 h 40 min" on a trail app comes from a speed model. Wrong models mean people out after dark.

🗺️ Accessibility maps

"Travel-time maps" show how far you can get in X hours across a landscape, for planning, ecology and logistics.

🌲 Parks & trail design

Which trails are slow, and why: steepness, rock, roots, vegetation? That guides maintenance and signage.

🛰️ Remote sensing

If satellites and lidar can predict trail difficulty, we get travel-time maps anywhere, even where nobody has mapped the trail details.

3 · The basics you need

Six ideas, each with a picture. If you only read one section of this page closely, make it this one.

3.1 · Slope, and Tobler's formula

Slope is rise over run, written as a percent. Climbing 10 m over 100 m of trail is +10%; descending the same is −10%. We always measure it in the direction the person is walking, so uphill is positive. A test in the code guards this sign; getting it backwards would silently flip every result.

Tobler (1993): speed = 6 · e−3.5 · |slope + 0.05| km/h. Fastest (6 km/h) at a gentle −5% downhill, falling off on both sides. It's used everywhere in GIS, but its constants came from old marching tables (Imhof, 1950), not from GPS tracks of real hikers.

3.2 · GPS traces and OpenStreetMap

OpenStreetMap (OSM) is the Wikipedia of maps. Volunteers draw roads and trails, and many upload their GPS traces: a list of points (latitude, longitude, time) recorded every 1–30 s while they walk, bike or drive.

When uploading, people pick a privacy level. That decides whether we can measure speed: speed needs timestamps in order. The OSM website's download service (the "API") strips the times from "public" traces. The lab's crawl of the original files keeps them, which is why the lab data added extra hikes.

3.3 · Map-matching: from wobbly dots to "which trail?"

GPS is noisy (a few meters, worse under trees). To know which trail someone was on, and so join its tags, slope and terrain, we "snap" the dots onto the OSM trail network. The method is an HMM map-matcher (hidden Markov model): it picks the most likely sequence of trails, not just the nearest trail for each dot.

Checked by hand: ~96% of points match. The failures are honest non-matches (canoe trips down a river, off-trail routes, unmapped paths), not wrong trails.

3.4 · Segments, stops and travel modes

✂️ 50 m segments

Each matched hike is cut into 50 m pieces along the trail. Each piece gets one speed (distance ÷ moving time) and one slope. We tested 25 m too: same curve, but 50 m pieces are timed by twice as many GPS points.

⏸️ Stops removed

Lunch, photos and views aren't "slow walking". A stop rule (still for ≥ 60 s within 10 m) removes about a quarter of the time. We tried four different rules; the results barely move.

🚶 🚴 Travel mode

Trails are shared with runners, mountain bikers and skiers. We separate them using traces on foot-only trails (bikes not allowed) as the reference, plus a "gravity" check: wheels and skis come downhill far faster than they climb.

3.5 · Remote sensing: lidar and satellites

Remote sensing = measuring the Earth from above. Two kinds matter here:

Airborne lidar. A plane fires laser pulses and times the echoes. Some echoes come from the tree tops, some from bushes, some from the ground. From them we get:

  • a DEM (digital elevation model): a grid of ground heights, at 1 m or 5 m;
  • roughness: rocks and roots on the trail;
  • vegetation density at knee-to-chest height (0.85–1.2 m, the band the STRIDE model uses);
  • canopy height.

Satellite embeddings. Google's "AlphaEarth" turns every 10 m pixel of the Earth into 64 numbers that summarize what's there (forest, rock, snow…). It's a free, pretrained summary we can attach to every trail segment.

3.6 · OSM trail tags

Mappers can attach tags to a trail. The useful ones for speed:

sac_scale · difficulty

hiking → mountain_hiking → demanding_mountain_hiking → alpine… In the White Mountains, at the same slope, "hiking"-grade trails were ~14% faster than "mountain_hiking" ones.

surface · ground type

ground, dirt, rock, gravel, paved… Present on about half of the trail segments.

The catch

About half the trails have no tags at all, and width is almost never tagged. That gap is why remote sensing matters.

4 · The research design

The plan is built around four questions and four "arms", competing ways to predict speed that differ only in what information they're allowed to see.

Q1

Measurement

What does slope vs speed really look like across thousands of real hikes? Is Tobler's shape right?

Q2 · the core

Remote sensing vs tags

Can lidar and satellite data stand in for missing trail tags, even in a new region?

Q3

Asymmetry

Is going down really faster than going up, and by how much? "Out-and-back" hikes are a natural experiment.

Q4 · stretch

Mode

Is a bike just "walking × a constant", or a different curve altogether?

The golden rule of evaluation: never test on what you trained on

7 · How the dataset is built

The same pipeline runs for every region, automatically, on the lab cluster. Numbers on the right are for the White Mountains (the first region) as an example.

Two data sources, one dataset. (1) The OSM API gives "trackable" and "identifiable" traces from all years, but only page by page: 5,000 points a page, ~5 s each, one request at a time. That's why downloads take hours: near highways we page through millions of car-logger points. (2) The lab's crawl of 1.5 million original trace files (2016–2022) adds "public" traces with their timestamps. The same hike from both sources is kept only once.

Quality checks (so we can trust it)

✅ Map-matching

~96% of GPS points match a trail; the 12 worst hikes were inspected by eye. Failures are correct non-matches.

✅ Stop rule

Four different stop rules remove 21–30% of the time, yet the peak speed moves by only ~0.1 km/h.

✅ Elevation

1 m and 5 m elevation give the same slopes (median difference 0.3 points of grade). Most regions sit on 1 m lidar; Olympic and the North Shore don't (flagged).

✅ Duplicates & group hikes

Re-uploads of the same hike are removed. Two phones on one hike count as one "outing", so we don't double-count people.

✅ Bad dates

A GPS clock bug put one 2007 hike in 1987. Flagged, so seasons aren't mislabeled.

⚠️ Known limits

OSM uploaders are enthusiasts, not average hikers. Some regions use lab data only so far. The API can't reach uploads older than ~2009 in the busiest tiles.

9 · What we've found

All before any remote sensing, from the T1 data alone. Each chart says what it means in plain words.

Finding 1 · Tobler is about 40% too fast at the peak, everywhere

Honest context: Campbell et al. (2019, 2022) found the same thing with Strava and AllTrails data from Utah and California (~4.1 km/h, a rounded "Lorentz" curve). So Finding 1 confirms earlier work, now across many regions. It's supporting context, not the paper's main claim (section 10 explains).

Finding 2 · Regions differ, and that's what remote sensing has to explain

Finding 3 · Downhill is only a bit faster

Finding 4 · Who you are vs where you walk

Finding 5 · No fatigue signal

Within a hike, speed (after slope) barely changes with hours walked: about −0.25% per hour.

Finding 6 · Tags carry information

White Mountains: "hiking"-grade trails are ~14% faster than "mountain_hiking" ones at the same slope; untagged trails ~11% slower. Remote sensing has to recover this.

Finding 7 · Winter isn't slower (?)

In the White Mountains, winter hikes weren't slower, maybe because packed snow covers the rocks. To test properly with satellite snow maps.

10 · Where we fit in the research world

A literature survey on 11 Oct changed the paper's focus. Earlier work covers pieces of this, but nobody has put all three together:

Tobler 1993The formula everyone uses (from 1950s tables)We test it on real hikes

So the paper's lead claim becomes: "Do we need a trail map? Predicting hiking speed in regions the model has never seen, from airborne lidar and satellite embeddings." The four arms are tested with leave-one-region-out, with Campbell, Wood and STRIDE as baselines next to Tobler, and with calibrated uncertainty.

11 · What's next

T2 · What remote sensing will add to every segment

tier 1

⛰️ Terrain (1 m lidar DEM)

Slope at 1 m, steepest pitch, roughness (rocks and roots: ground minus a 2 m-smoothed ground, STRIDE's definition).

tier 1

🌲 Canopy height

Meta's free 1 m canopy-height map (covers Canada too). Plus land cover and canopy cover (NLCD, 30 m).

tier 1

🛰️ Satellite embeddings

AlphaEarth: 64 numbers per 10 m pixel summarizing the landscape, matched to the hike's year. A free, pretrained summary of what's there.

tier 2

🌿 Understory density

Share of lidar echoes at 0.85–1.2 m height (bushes at knee-to-chest level), from USGS point clouds on the cluster.

tier 2

❄️ Snow on the hike date

Daily satellite snow maps (MODIS, SNODAS). Something no map can have: conditions on that day.

tier 3

🖼️ Aerial images

Image features from 0.6 m aerial photos (NAIP), RasterNet-style. Stretch goal / follow-on paper.

T3 · The models and the test

Models

Gradient-boosted trees (a strong, honest baseline), and Tobler's formula with its constants learned from terrain. That gives readable results like "k ≈ 2.4 on rocky trails, 1.7 on smooth ones".

Scores

Speed errors per segment, whole-hike time errors, and calibration: does a predicted "80% range" really contain 80% of hikes?

Gate 2 (~13 Nov)

Does the remote-sensing arm get close to the tags arm in held-out regions? Either answer is publishable; the gap size is the finding.

The paper: 4 pages, IEEE format

If a mentor asks… (short answers)

"Why is the peak speed so low?" Several reasons:

  • Campbell found the same (~4.1 km/h).
  • Our number holds under four stop rules and with 1 m elevation.
  • The data match Tobler on steep grades, so speeds and slopes aren't broken.
  • Real trails are rough, and Tobler's constants came from 1950s marching tables.

"How do you know which trail someone was on?" HMM map-matching. ~96% of points match, and we inspected the worst cases by eye.

"Aren't segments from one hike correlated?" Yes. Every interval resamples whole outings, and every test holds out whole hikes, map squares or regions, never single points.

"What's new compared with Campbell and Wood?" Testing in unseen regions; remote sensing vs tags head-to-head; calibrated uncertainty; same-hiker asymmetry at scale.

"Why so many regions?" You asked for it, and it enables the leave-one-region-out test, the paper's strongest claim.
