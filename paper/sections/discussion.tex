\textbf{Why tags transfer less well.} Not because tag values mean
different things in different places: within the same hike, harder
sac\_scale grades are slower in every region with enough tagged trail
(mountain\_hiking vs hiking $-5\%$, 10 of 10 regions; harder grades
$-11\%$, 8 of 8). Tags are mapped unevenly (sac\_scale is on 1--96\% of
walked trail, by region), but in unseen regions the additive tag model
scores about the same on tagged and untagged segments; the loss is mostly
in the boosted trees ($+1.5\% \to +0.4\%$), as if they fit region-specific
tag combinations. Terrain exists on every trail, and its effect has the
same sign in all 17 regions (steppiest vs smoothest fifth, $-12\%$).

\textbf{What terrain captures.} Uneven grade within the segment, such as
rock steps and short pitches, plus difficulty beyond grade. In the additive
model its gain is largest on descents and level trail ($+2.3$ to $+2.8\%$)
and smallest on steep climbs ($+0.8\%$), as if footing sets the pace going
down and effort going up; the trees, though, gain $+2.6\%$ on steep climbs.
The signal is the trail's elevation profile, not micro-relief: 1~m lidar
adds little over the 5~m grid, and STRIDE's off-path roughness and
understory terms add nothing on trails or make it worse.

\textbf{Use.} The terrain-adjusted function, Eq.~(\ref{eq:terrain}), needs only
a DEM. Fitted on the other
16 regions, it gives a pace for all 3,369~km of White Mountains trail,
77\% of which no trace in our data covers. It travels less far than the full terrain model, though
($+0.6\%$ with a whole zone held out, $-0.3\%$ between East and West).

\textbf{Limits.} Our hikers are OSM uploaders, not a random sample. Sparse
traces read slow (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), as hiking functions depend on the
sampling resolution~\cite{goodwin2025application}; distance along the
mapped trail would raise speeds by about 1--3\%. Per-segment gains are a
few percent because GPS noise and the hiker dominate.
Weather~\cite{thornton2022daymet} (a factor in~\cite{huang2026modeling})
slows our hikers on hot days ($-3\%$
above 25$^\circ$C) and with snow cover ($-4\%$), but neither weather
($-0.0\%$) nor date and time of day improves predictions in new regions,
and once a hiker's pace on the day is known, weather adds nothing.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Across 17 North American regions, terrain from
elevation data predicts hiking speed in regions a model has never seen
better than OSM tags, which keep only part of their gain there; vegetation
adds little, and satellite embeddings help mainly where the model was
trained. Tobler's function is too fast on gentle trail and overstates the
speed-up downhill. The terrain-adjusted function keeps part of the terrain
gain and needs only a DEM, though a hiker's own pace matters most.
