How long a walk will take matters for search and rescue, for wildland
firefighters' escape routes~\cite{campbell2017lidar} and for the times
trail apps show. A common starting point is Tobler's hiking
function~\cite{tobler1993}, $v = 6\exp(-3.5\,|s + 0.05|)$~km/h on slope
$s$, which peaks at 6~km/h on a gentle descent. It is still in use, even
for traverse planning in planetary exploration~\cite{goodwin2025application}.

Crowdsourced GPS tracks show that the function is too fast and too peaked,
and that speeds at one slope vary
widely~\cite{campbell2019crowdsourced,campbell2022variability}. Newer models
add covariates: OSM terrain classes and lidar-measured obstruction in
England and Wales~\cite{wood2023hiking}; boosted trees on 16 factors,
weather included, in Hong Kong~\cite{huang2026modeling}; a latent walking
speed predicted from Sentinel-2 imagery in one Croatian
range~\cite{seric2022modeling}; the hiker's pace on the first part of a
hike~\cite{asako2024hikingtte}. STRIDE~\cite{campbell2024stride} models
walking speed from lidar slope, understory
density~\cite{campbell2018understory} and ground roughness, calibrated on
timed field walks. On roads, speeds and their spread have been learned from
overhead imagery and
lidar~\cite{hadzic2020rasternet,workman2024probabilistic}.

Wood et al.~\cite{wood2023hiking} test with 10-fold cross-validation and
Huang et al.~\cite{huang2026modeling} with a random split, so their test
tracks come from the areas they trained on. Yet predictions matter most where
nobody has recorded a hike: in the White Mountains, one of our best-sampled
regions, 77\% of the 3,369~km of mapped trail has no trace in our data. Two
sources could describe such trails. On the trail map, OSM mappers tag
difficulty (sac\_scale), surface and visibility, but unevenly: sac\_scale
is on 1--96\% of walked trail, depending on the region. Remote sensing
reaches every trail, tagged or not: the USGS 3D Elevation Program collects
airborne lidar across the United States~\cite{sugarbaker2017status}, and
satellite embeddings summarize imagery worldwide~\cite{brown2025alphaearth}.
To our knowledge, no study has tested which of the two works in regions a
model has never seen.

We test it on public OpenStreetMap GPS traces~\cite{osm} from 17 mountain
regions of the United States and Canada. We contribute (i)~a dataset of
290,640 walking traversals of 50~m trail segments (1,992 outings) with
slope, OSM tags and remote-sensing covariates (Sec.~\ref{sec:data});
(ii)~a transfer test that holds out whole regions (also with their
hikers), zones or half the continent and scores predictive distributions with the continuous ranked
probability score (CRPS)~\cite{gneiting2007strictly}, against published
hiking functions (Sec.~\ref{sec:methods}); and (iii)~the finding that
terrain measured from elevation data transfers ($+2.1$ to $+2.8\%$ CRPS
skill over a refitted slope curve in unseen regions, ahead of tags in 16 of
17 with additive models), while tags add $+0.4$ to $+0.9\%$ and neither vegetation, from 30~m
maps or lidar point clouds, nor satellite embeddings match it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). We also give a terrain-adjusted hiking function with one DEM-derived input
($+1.0\%$).
