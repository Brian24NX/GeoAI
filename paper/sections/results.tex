\textbf{Tobler's function fails everywhere.} Walking speed peaks near
4.2~km/h on level to gently descending trail, not at 6~km/h
(Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}a): Tobler's function is about 20\% too fast within
$\pm$10\% grade and 18\% too slow on climbs steeper than 25\%, and in
unseen regions it scores $-12.5\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:skill}), worse in all
17. Campbell et al.'s published median curve~\cite{campbell2022variability}
is about 20\% too fast on climbs steeper than 25\%; it ties our refit on
average ($-0.5\%$) but ranges by region from $-3.8\%$ (White Mountains) to
$+5.8\%$ (Shenandoah). Every model stays calibrated in unseen regions
(90\% intervals cover about 89\% of segments).

\textbf{Downhill is faster, but less than Tobler implies.} For the same
hiker on the same trail (2,763 out-and-back pairs from 1,145 outings;
Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}b), descending is 8\% faster than climbing at
3--10\% grade and 22--23\% above 20\%, not the 42\% Tobler implies. Our
pooled curve implies nearly the same ratios, so its asymmetry is not an
artifact of who walks which slopes.

\textbf{Map tags do not transfer.} OSM tags improve on the slope curve for
new hikers in known regions ($+1.3\%$ / $+1.5\%$, additive / trees) but
lose a third to three quarters of that in unseen regions ($+0.9\%$
[0.1, 1.7] / $+0.4\%$ [$-1.1$, 1.6]).

\textbf{Terrain transfers.} Terrain from elevation gains nearly as much in
unseen regions as within them, $+2.1\%$ [1.5, 3.1] / $+2.8\%$ [1.8, 4.1]. It
beats tags in 16 of 17 held-out regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:skill}b), and even
a slope curve refitted on the held-out region's own other hikers
($+1.2\%$). Terrain minus tags is $+0.7$ to $+2.2$ points (95\% interval),
above zero in every bootstrap draw. About 60\% of the gain is uneven grade
inside the segment that one average slope hides (the hidden-climbing
control alone gives $+1.2\%$ / $+1.6\%$). Terrain is neither a region
proxy (within regions it adds $\sim$1.9 points to a learned offset per
region) nor a hiker proxy (it adds $\sim$2.3 points to the hiker's own
pace).

No other remote-sensing input transfers as well. 30~m land cover and
canopy add nothing. AlphaEarth embeddings help within regions ($+1.4\%$ /
$+2.6\%$) but less, and less reliably, in unseen ones ($+1.1\%$).
Vegetation from the lidar point clouds helps within a region (White
Mountains, new 10~km squares: $+3.6\% \to +5.0\%$ added to terrain) but
adds only $+0.2$ points [$-0.2$, 0.5] to terrain in unseen regions.
Published STRIDE~\cite{campbell2024stride} scores $-16.8\%$; refitted,
$+0.2\%$.

\textbf{Robustness.} The terrain gain holds for newer hikes (trained to
2019, tested on 2020 on: $+2.2\%$ / $+2.9\%$; tags $+0.9\%$), at
every GPS recording rate (traces every $\le$2~s, 2--10~s and $>$10~s:
$+1.7\%$, $+2.4\%$ and $+1.9\%$ in unseen regions, additive) and with no
neighbouring region in training: with a whole zone held out, $+1.9\%$ /
$+2.7\%$ (tags $+0.7\%$ / $-0.1\%$); from West to East and back, $+2.6\%$
/ $+3.3\%$ (tags $+1.5\%$ / $-0.3\%$).

\textbf{Whole hikes: the hiker dominates.} Over 1,354 hikes in held-out
regions, the median error in moving time after the first kilometre is
13.6\% for the slope curve and 13.0\% with terrain, but 10.0\% with the
hiker's pace on that kilometre and 9.6\% with both (CRPS skill $+8.4\%$;
with terrain $+10.7\%$ / $+11.6\%$).

\textbf{Other travel modes.} The walking curve times a constant
($\times$2.5) predicts runners as well as their own curve, but not bikes
and skis ($\sim$4$\times$ walking speed downhill, $\sim$1.6$\times$
uphill; own curve 3.8\% better).
